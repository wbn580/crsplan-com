## Who Needs to Report Under CRS?

The Common Reporting Standard (CRS) requires financial institutions to report information on accounts held by tax residents of participating jurisdictions. The reporting obligation falls on the financial institution, not directly on the individual taxpayer, but cross-border residents must ensure their information is accurate and complete for proper reporting.

A determining factor in whether an account is reportable is the account holder's tax residency. For high-net-worth individuals with ties to multiple countries, correct determination of tax residency is essential, as it drives which jurisdictions receive information about the account.

## Classifying Accounts and Identifying Reportable Assets

Financial institutions are required to conduct due diligence to identify reportable accounts. Accounts held by individuals that are located in a CRS-participating jurisdiction are subject to review, and the institution must determine the tax residency of the account holder based on the information gathered.

![A professional reviewing financial reporting documents with a calculator](https://img.crsplan.com/跨境法务-CRS合规/crs-2026-09-06-4d2da25b14-image-02-2026-1880x1253.jpg)


Common asset types that appear in cross-border reporting include overseas real estate, insurance policies, and trusts. It's important to note that the reporting is account-based: financial accounts such as custodial accounts, deposit accounts, and certain investment entities are reportable. However, not all assets are directly reportable. For instance, an overseas property held directly is not a financial account, but if it is held through a company or trust that qualifies as a financial institution, that structure may be reportable.

## Reporting Timelines and Information Submitted

CRS reporting follows an annual cycle. Financial institutions must review their accounts, collect the required information, and report to their local tax authority by specific deadlines, which vary by jurisdiction. The reported information includes the account holder's name, address, tax identification number, and account balance or value, along with details of income and gross proceeds (if applicable).

For correct reporting, the financial institution must rely on self-certification forms and other documentation provided by the account holder. Accuracy of these details is crucial, as errors can trigger mismatches and delays in information exchange.

## Common Errors and How to Avoid Them

Several common mistakes can occur in CRS reporting, including:

![A checklist titled 'Global Tax Compliance' with pens on a desk](https://img.crsplan.com/跨境法务-CRS合规/crs-2026-09-06-4d2da25b14-image-03-2026-1733x1300.jpg)


- Incorrect tax residency determination, especially when an individual has multiple ties to different countries.
- Missing or incomplete self-certifications, leading to improper reporting of accounts.
- Failure to update records when an individual's circumstances change (e.g., moving to a new country).
- Errors in account balances or values, which can arise from conversion issues or unreported income.

To avoid these, it is recommended to use a structured review process that includes verifying all data points against source documents, checking for consistency across accounts, and confirming that each account is correctly classified. For high-net-worth individuals with complex structures, regular reviews and up-to-date documentation are essential.

## Next Steps for Tax Professionals and Client Advisors

For advisers assisting cross-border clients, the practical steps include:

1. Gather and review self-certification forms for all accounts.
2. Determine tax residency for each client based on established rules.
3. Ensure accurate account classification and balance reporting.
4. Submit reports through the proper channels by the jurisdiction's deadline.
5. Implement a review process to catch errors before submission.
