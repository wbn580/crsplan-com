Common CRS Data Errors and How to Correct Them: A Case-Based Review

Introduction

Commonwealth and cross-border residents and their tax advisors often face compliance risks due to errors in CRS reporting. This review, based on practical cases, highlights frequent mistakes involving account balances, tax residency status, and account holder types, and outlines a step-by-step approach to proactive correction and supplementary filing.

Frequent Data Errors in CRS Reporting

Account Balance Misreporting

A recurring issue is the incorrect reporting of account balances. Errors may arise from using the wrong valuation date, including or excluding accrued interest, or failing to convert foreign currency at the correct exchange rate. For instance, a case showed a taxpayer misreporting the balance because they omitted interest that had accrued but not yet been credited. Such discrepancies can trigger mismatches in automatic exchange of information.

Tax Residency Misidentification

Another common error is the incorrect determination of tax residency status. Individuals often assume residency based on nationality or where they work, but the CRS rules require an analysis of tie-breaker rules and local tax laws. A case in point involves a dual resident who reported only one jurisdiction, leading to incomplete reporting to the other country. Entities, too, face confusion about whether they are considered residents for tax purposes, especially when management and control are split across borders.

Account Holder Type Errors

Errors also occur in classifying the type of account holder. For example, a trust might be incorrectly reported as a passive financial institution, or an individual might be reported as an entity due to a mistaken interpretation of the term "financial account." This misclassification can lead to incorrect reporting of the account's controlling persons, which has significant compliance implications.

Analyzing the Root Causes

These errors often stem from a lack of understanding of the specific rules applicable to different account holder types, reliance on incomplete data, and misinterpretation of residency criteria. Taxpayers and advisors may also underestimate the importance of keeping accurate and updated information throughout the year.

Step-by-Step Correction and Supplementary Filing Process

To mitigate risks, a structured approach is recommended:

  1. Identify the Error: Review past CRS filings and compare them with the actual account information, residency status, and holder type. This includes verifying the valuation date, currency conversion, and inclusion of all relevant income.

  2. Assess the Impact: Determine which jurisdictions are affected and whether the error leads to under- or over-reporting. This step helps prioritize corrective actions.

  3. Consult a Qualified Advisor: Given the complexity of CRS rules, seeking advice from a tax professional with international expertise is prudent. They can help interpret the rules and ensure compliance.

  4. Prepare Corrected Data: Gather all necessary documents and recalculate the account balance, determine the correct tax residency, and correctly classify the account holder type. Ensure that all supporting evidence is retained.

  5. File a Correction or Supplementary Report: Use the appropriate procedures in the relevant jurisdiction to submit the corrected information. This may involve amending the original report or filing a new one, depending on local requirements.

  6. Update Internal Records and Processes: Implement changes to prevent recurrence, such as improving data collection and verification procedures before filing.

Conclusion

Proactive identification and correction of CRS reporting errors are essential for maintaining compliance and avoiding penalties. By understanding common pitfalls and following a systematic correction process, cross-border residents and their advisors can reduce compliance risk.
