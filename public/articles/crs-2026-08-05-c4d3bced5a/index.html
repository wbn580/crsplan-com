CRS Filing Pitfalls for High-Net-Worth and Cross-Border Residents

Introduction

Under the Common Reporting Standard (CRS), high-net-worth individuals and cross-border residents face complex reporting obligations. Mistakes in account classification, information disclosure, and the treatment of non-financial assets can trigger penalties and back taxes. This guide outlines common pitfalls and provides practical steps for tax advisors and legal assistants to help clients stay compliant.

Common Misconceptions and Errors

Misclassifying Account Types

One frequent error is the misclassification of financial accounts. For CRS purposes, accounts are categorized as depository, custodial, equity, or cash-value insurance accounts. Incorrectly labelling a custodial account as a depository account can lead to inaccurate reporting and potential non-compliance.

Omitting Reportable Information

Another common mistake is the omission of required information, such as tax identification numbers (TINs), dates of birth, or even entire accounts. Failing to include a jointly held account or a trust interest can result in incomplete reporting and increased scrutiny.

Underestimating Non-Financial Assets

Many taxpayers overlook that certain non-financial assets held through investment entities may be reportable. For example, if a client holds a controlling interest in a company that is a passive non-financial entity, that entity itself may need to be reported. Misunderstanding the rules around investment entities can lead to significant reporting gaps.

Practical Case Scenarios

Consider a cross-border resident who holds a cash-value life insurance policy but mistakenly believes it is not a reportable account. In some jurisdictions, cash-value insurance contracts are reportable under CRS. Similarly, a client with a trust where they are a controlling person may not realize that the trust's accounts must be reported, even if the trust is not resident in the client's country.

Self-Audit Checklist for Tax Professionals

To help clients avoid common pitfalls, use this checklist during your review: - Verify the account classification for every financial account. - Ensure all required data points (name, address, TIN, birth date) are collected and accurate. - Identify all reportable accounts, including those held jointly, through trusts, or via investment entities. - Assess whether any non-financial assets trigger reporting through an investment entity. - Confirm that all controlling persons of entities are properly identified. - Double-check that no accounts are omitted due to low balances or type.

Steps to Correct CRS Filing Errors

If errors are discovered, take the following corrective actions: - Document the error and the corrected information. - Notify the relevant financial institution and provide updated self-certification forms. - If the previous filing was already submitted, work with the tax authority to file an amended report. - Implement internal controls to prevent recurrence, such as periodic training for staff and a pre-filing checklist.

Conclusion

CRS compliance requires vigilance to avoid costly mistakes. By understanding common pitfalls and following a systematic review, tax professionals can help clients stay compliant and avoid penalties. Use this guide as a foundation for your own compliance procedures.
